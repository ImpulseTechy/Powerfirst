Power First

How to choose and build the right power supply for any project

For B.Tech students and makers. Every circuit buildable on perfboard, every part orderable in India, every claim measured on a real bench.

Why this book exists

Students don’t fail at the equations. They fail when the calculated design meets real hardware.

There are excellent books on power supply design. They teach loop compensation, phase margin, transformer core geometry and winding arrangement. Not one of them helps the student whose ESP32 resets when the servo moves.

That gap is where almost every real failure lives. A calculated inductor value that maps onto five thousand catalogue parts. A capacitor chosen by capacitance when ESR was what mattered. A regulator sold as 1 A that thermally delivers 300 mA. A supply that behaves perfectly on the bench and collapses the moment a radio transmits.

This book teaches the part that gets skipped: how to work out what your project actually needs, choose parts you can buy today, build it on perfboard, and prove with measurements that it works.

Who it’s for

  • B.Tech students who can build circuits but have never really read a datasheet
  • Makers whose projects work on USB and fail on a battery
  • Anyone who has blamed their code for a problem that was really their power supply

What it deliberately leaves out

No transformer winding. No loop compensation. No PCB layout — that is book two. You will learn to select components, not design magnetics, and to measure a supply rather than compensate one.

The eight steps

Every chapter applies the same sequence. By the end you will run it without thinking.

00Profile the loadEvery rail. Average current, peak current, how long the peak lasts, how often, and how much noise it tolerates.
01Write the specInput voltage range, output voltage, average and peak current, ripple limit, efficiency target, thermal ceiling.
02Choose the sourceMains, adapter, USB, or which battery chemistry and how many cells in series.
03Choose the topologyLinear, LDO, buck, boost or buck-boost. One decision table settles it.
04Choose the partsIC, inductor, capacitors, MOSFET, diode — each from the handful of datasheet numbers that actually decide.
05Check the heatDissipation and temperature rise, calculated before you spend a rupee. This step kills more designs than any other.
06Build it on perfboardShort fat wires on the high-current loop, star ground, bulk capacitance at the load end.
07Measure and prove itThe six measurements. If you have not measured it, you do not know that it works.

Chapters go live as they are written and measured. Nothing is published until the circuit in it has been built.

Part 0 · Chapter 1Published

Six failures, one cause

Here are six messages from the channel. They arrived separately, from six different people, about six different projects. Five of the six people believed they had a software problem.

All six are the same problem.

1.1 The six

“My ESP32 keeps restarting whenever it connects to WiFi. Serial monitor shows brownout detector was triggered. Code is fine, I tested it.”

The code is fine. When an ESP32 turns on its radio, current draw jumps from around 80 mA to peaks of 300–500 mA in a few microseconds. If it is running from a laptop USB port through a thin cable, or from an AMS1117 that is already near its thermal limit, the 3.3 V rail sags. The chip has a brownout detector watching that rail, and when it dips below roughly 2.8 V the chip does exactly what it was designed to do — it resets. The error message is not a bug report. It is the chip telling you, accurately, that you did not feed it.

“When my servo moves, the OLED display goes blank and I have to restart.”

An MG996R servo draws about 500 mA while turning and up to 2.5 A if it stalls. Your OLED wants 20 mA and shares the same 5 V rail and the same thin wire. When the servo starts, the rail dips, and the display controller loses its state on the I²C bus. The display is not faulty. It is downstream of a motor.

“My SIM800 restarts whenever it sends an SMS. I am using a 5V 2A adapter.”

The adapter can deliver two amps — slowly. The module wants two amps for 577 microseconds, two hundred and sixteen times a second. Those are completely different requirements, and almost nothing you can buy is labelled for the second one. This one gets a whole chapter.

“My 7805 gets so hot I cannot touch it. Is it damaged?”

It is not damaged, it is doing precisely what a linear regulator does. Feed it 12 V, take 5 V out at half an amp, and the difference has nowhere to go but heat:

P = (12 V − 5 V) × 0.5 A = 3.5 W

A bare TO-220 package can shed roughly one watt before it becomes untouchable. You asked it to shed three and a half. The part is fine; the arithmetic was never done.

“My motor driver works when powered from USB but not from a 9V battery.”

A 9 V PP3 battery has an internal resistance of roughly 1.7 Ω when fresh, and considerably more once used. Ask it for 500 mA and you lose most of a volt inside the battery itself before any current reaches your circuit. That little rectangular battery is one of the weakest power sources you can buy, and it is on the shelf of every electronics shop, which is why it ends up in so many projects that fail.

“It was working perfectly on breadboard. After I soldered everything it stopped working.”

Something in the layout changed. The loop got longer, the wire got thinner, or a decoupling capacitor moved away from the pin it was protecting. Nothing in the schematic changed at all — and that is exactly why it is so confusing.

1.2 What they have in common

In every one of these, the voltage went somewhere it should not have — for a moment too short to see.

Not one of them is a component failure. Not one is a code bug. In each case a rail moved outside the window its load needed, briefly, and something downstream reacted correctly to a condition its designer anticipated.

1.3 Why you did not see it

Because you measured with a multimeter, and a multimeter cannot see this.

A digital multimeter samples a few times per second and reports an average. The dips in these stories last between 200 µs and a few milliseconds. Averaged into a one-second window they move the reading by a few hundredths of a volt. Your meter says 3.29 V, which looks perfect, and it is not lying — it is answering a different question from the one you needed to ask.

The single most expensive habit in this hobby

Trusting a multimeter to tell you whether a power supply is healthy. It tells you the average. Almost every failure in this book is about what happens for a fraction of a millisecond, and the average is exactly where that information goes to die.

This is why chapter 2 is about your bench before chapter 3 is about your circuit. You cannot debug what you cannot see, and the research is unambiguous that not knowing where to measure — or what a correct waveform even looks like — is the single biggest thing standing between students and working power supplies.

1.4 What this book does instead

Most power supply teaching starts with a topology. Here is a buck converter, here is the duty cycle equation, calculate L, calculate C, simulate it, done.

That order is backwards, and it is why the calculations stop matching reality the moment real parts are involved. You cannot choose a topology before you know what the load does. You cannot choose a part before you know how much heat you can get rid of. And you cannot claim any of it works until you have put a probe on it.

So this book runs in the order the work actually happens — the eight steps. You will not see a converter topology until chapter 13, and by then you will already be able to say exactly what your project needs, which is the part everybody skips.

Decision rules — chapter 1

  • When something resets, browns out, flickers or behaves differently on battery than on USB, suspect power first.
  • A multimeter measures the average. Failures live in the moments the average hides.
  • The load decides the supply. Never the other way round.
  • A regulator that runs hot is not faulty. It is telling you the arithmetic was skipped.

1.5 Exercises

  1. Take a project you have built. Write down every part, and next to it the current it draws when idle and the current it draws at its busiest. Where you do not know, write “don’t know” — that list of blanks is chapter 3.
  2. An LM7812 drops 24 V to 12 V at 300 mA. How many watts does it dissipate? Would you be able to hold it?
  3. Find one comment or forum post describing a project that “works on USB but not on battery.” Which of the six stories above is it?

Pulsed loads: powering a GSM or LTE module from a battery

26.1 The symptom

“Sir my SIM800 keeps restarting whenever it sends SMS. I am using 5V 2A adapter only. Everything else works fine. Please help.”— the most common power question on the channel

This module has never had a code problem. It has a supply that can deliver two amps slowly, and a load that wants two amps in half a millisecond. Those are not the same requirement, and almost nothing you can buy is labelled for the second one.

26.2 The number on the box is not the number you need

Open the SIM800 hardware design guide. It says the supply “must be able to provide sufficient current up to 2 A.” True, but incomplete. Two pages later the timing diagram tells you what actually happens: the transmit burst lasts 577 µs inside a 4.615 ms frame, and the permitted droop on VBAT is 350 mV.

duty cycle = 577 µs ÷ 4 615 µs = 12.5 %
average during transmit ≈ 2 A × 0.125 + idle ≈ 250–350 mA

So the module draws about a third of an amp on average, and spikes to two amps for roughly half a millisecond, two hundred and sixteen times a second. You do not need a 2 A continuous supply. You need a supply that survives a 2 A spike.

And if you are using a SIM7670 or A7670?

Different load entirely, despite the similar name. The SIM7672X series wants 3.4–4.2 V at VBAT, typically 3.8 V — not 5 V — and LTE-FDD B1 transmit at 23 dBm draws about 574 mA typical. Its design guide asks for only ≥100 µF of bulk. The savage 2 A burst belongs to the 2G parts. The “5 V 2 A” printed on your breakout board is the recommended adapter, sized with headroom — it was never the module’s draw.

26.3 Where the current actually comes from

The regulator supplies the average. The capacitor supplies the burst.

During those 577 µs the regulator cannot possibly respond — its control loop is far too slow. The charge has to come from a capacitor sitting next to the module, and the voltage it loses while supplying that charge is:

ΔV = (I_peak − I_reg) × t_burst ÷ C + I_peak × ESR

Two terms, and beginners only ever think about the first.

Work it, with a 350 mV budget

The ESR term first, because it is the one that ruins designs. A generic 1000 µF 16 V electrolytic from the local market has an ESR around 0.15 Ω.

2 A × 0.15 Ω = 300 mV — instantly, before the capacitor has discharged at all

Eighty-six percent of your entire budget, spent on a parasitic. The capacitance was never the problem. Swap to a low-ESR part at roughly 0.05 Ω and the same 2 A costs you 100 mV, leaving 250 mV for the capacitor to actually work with.

Now size the capacitance, assuming your regulator can hold up 1 A of the 2 A peak:

C = (2 A − 1 A) × 577 µs ÷ 0.25 V = 2 308 µF → use 2 200–3 300 µF, low ESR

Plus 10 µF and 100 nF ceramics right at the pin for the fast edges, and the 5.1 V zener the datasheet recommends.

Check it against the manufacturer

SIMCom’s A7670 guide says: ≥300 µF if your supply can source the full peak, and ≥1000 µF if it cannot. Our arithmetic lands in the same place. That is the point of this chapter — the datasheet number is not magic, it is this equation with their assumptions plugged in. Once you can derive it, you can adapt it when your regulator, your budget or your burst is different.

26.4 The battery is part of the power supply

You can get every capacitor right and still fail, because the cell itself has resistance and the same burst flows through it.

For a pulsed load, choose the cell by internal resistance, not by capacity. A 3000 mAh cell that sags 300 mV is worse than a 2000 mAh cell that sags 50 mV, and the mAh number on the wrapper tells you nothing about which one you are holding.

26.5 Five architectures, compared

ArchitectureVerdict
A1S Li-ion straight to VBAT, with bulk capacitanceRecommended. The cell’s 3.0–4.2 V window is the module’s 3.4–4.2 V window. No conversion, no loss, no switching noise. You lose a little capacity at the discharge tail because the module cuts out near 3.5 V under load. Needs protection and a genuinely low-ESR bulk cap.
B1S Li-ion → boost to 5 V → module’s own regulatorDouble conversion for nothing. Worse, 2 A out at 5 V needs about 3.2 A from the cell — an SX1308 at ₹30 or an MT3608 cannot come close.
C2S Li-ion → buck to 4.0 VWorks, and LM2596 at ₹44 is SIMCom’s own reference part. But 2S brings balancing, a BMS and a 2S charger. Choose this when the project needs other rails too.
D2S → 7805 → moduleFails twice. The 7805 needs ≥7 V in and a discharged 2S pack sits at 6.0 V. And at the peak it burns 3.4 V × 2 A = 6.8 W across the pass element.
ECertified adapter → LM2596 → 4.0 VCorrect for a bench prototype. Not battery operated, so it does not answer the question — but build this first to prove the circuit before you add the battery.

26.6 What most people draw, and what actually works

26.7 Build it on perfboard

  • Bulk capacitor within 20 mm of the VBAT pin. At the battery end it does almost nothing.
  • The loop from capacitor, through VBAT, through GND and back to the capacitor is the loop. Keep it short and use 20 AWG. Not jumper wire.
  • Star ground at the cell negative. The module’s ground return does not pass through your microcontroller’s ground.
  • Ceramics closest to the pin, electrolytic just behind them. Small and fast in front, big and slow behind.
  • Never power the module from an Arduino’s 5 V pin. That regulator cannot source the burst, and now two things brown out instead of one.

26.8 Measure it

Scope setup

Probe on VBAT, ground spring at the module’s own ground pin. DC coupled, 100 mV/div, timebase 200 µs/div, trigger on the falling edge about 100 mV below the resting voltage. Send an SMS.

You are looking for a train of dips spaced 4.6 ms apart. Pass condition: each dip is under 350 mV and the rail recovers fully before the next one arrives. If the dips get progressively deeper across the burst train, your capacitor is not recharging in time — the regulator is the limit, not the capacitor.

Take this trace twice, once with the original supply and once with the fix, and put them side by side. That single pair of images is the most persuasive thing in the chapter.

26.9 Decision rules

Keep these

  • Read the burst spec, not the headline current. Peak, duration and repetition rate.
  • The regulator supplies the average. The capacitor supplies the burst.
  • Budget ESR before capacitance. It can spend your entire allowance before the capacitor does any work.
  • If the load’s voltage window already matches the battery’s, do not convert at all.
  • For pulsed loads, choose cells by internal resistance, not capacity.

26.10 Common mistakes

  • A 2 A adapter feeding thin wires with the capacitor at the wrong end — the original question, exactly.
  • A generic high-ESR electrolytic, chosen on capacitance alone.
  • Powering the module from the Arduino’s 5 V pin.
  • A coin cell or AAA pack for anything containing a radio.
  • Assuming a SIM7670 behaves like a SIM800 because both say “GSM module” on the listing.

26.11 Exercises

  1. Your regulator can hold 1.5 A of the 2 A peak and you allow 300 mV of droop. The capacitor’s ESR is 0.04 Ω. What capacitance do you need?
  2. A cell sags 250 mV at 2 A. What is its internal resistance, and would you use it for this design?
  3. Architecture A cuts out around 3.5 V under load instead of 3.0 V. Using a discharge curve, roughly what percentage of capacity does that cost — and is it worth the efficiency you gain?
