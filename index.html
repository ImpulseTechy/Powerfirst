Your specification

Change anything and the answer updates.

The lowest voltage your source will ever give — not the number printed on the label. This is what decides whether a regulator can work at all, because if the input falls too far the output simply follows it down.

A 12 V adapter can sit at 11.4 V. A 1S Li-ion sold as 3.7 V falls to 3.0 V when nearly empty. A 12 V lead-acid battery reaches about 10.5 V. Always use the worst case — that is the moment your circuit fails.

The highest voltage your source will ever reach. It decides two things: whether your parts survive it, and how much heat a linear regulator makes — because the waste is (Vin − Vout) × I, and that is worst at the top of the range.

A 12 V adapter can reach 12.6 V with nothing connected. A fully charged 1S Li-ion is 4.2 V, not 3.7 V. A car’s electrical system hits 14.4 V with the engine running.

The voltage your load needs. Most digital parts want 3.3 V or 5 V, within a few percent.

But check whether your load truly needs a fixed voltage or accepts a range — many modules accept a range, and if that range covers your battery’s range you may not need a regulator at all. A cellular module wanting 3.4–4.2 V is almost exactly a Li-ion cell.

What your circuit draws most of the time, measured over seconds rather than microseconds. This sets how much heat is produced and how long a battery lasts.

It is not the sum of every datasheet maximum — real parts spend most of their life idle. An ESP32 averages around 80 mA even though it peaks far higher. Measure it with a USB power meter if you can; otherwise use the datasheet’s typical figures, not the maximums.

The highest current your circuit draws, even for an instant. This decides whether your rail sags far enough to reset something, and how much capacitance you need.

Peaks are usually several times the average. A WiFi radio jumps to about 500 mA when it transmits. A servo pulls 2.5 A if it stalls. A 2G module takes 2 A mid-burst. Getting this number wrong is the most common reason a circuit works on the bench and fails in use.

How long a single peak lasts, in microseconds. 1000 µs = 1 ms.

This matters because a regulator’s control loop needs tens of microseconds to react. A short spike is supplied almost entirely by a capacitor, not by the regulator — so duration changes the answer completely.

A GSM transmit burst is 577 µs. A WiFi burst runs a few milliseconds (~5000 µs). A servo moving takes hundreds of milliseconds. Short and violent needs capacitance; long and heavy needs a bigger supply.

The air temperature around the part — not room temperature in the abstract. Every heat calculation starts here: junction temperature is ambient plus the rise the part creates, and silicon gives up near 125 °C.

Use 35 °C for a typical Indian room. If the board lives inside a sealed enclosure where air cannot move, 50–60 °C is realistic — and every other number on this page shifts with it.

The ideas behind the answers

Sixteen things that decide whether a power supply works. Every result above links down to the one it depends on.

The heat equation

A linear regulator does not remove the extra voltage — it burns it. P = (Vin − Vout) × I, every watt turning into heat inside one small package.

How hot that gets depends on thermal resistance, in °C per watt. A bare TO-220 in still air is about 65 °C/W. So one watt is already a 65 °C rise, and on a 35 °C day that is a junction at 100 °C. That is where the rule of thumb comes from: about one watt is all a bare TO-220 can take. Above that you need a heatsink, or you need to stop using a linear regulator.

Why a “1 A” part is not a 1 A part

The current on the front of a datasheet is what the silicon can pass, not what the package can cool. Those are different limits and the smaller one wins.

An AMS1117 is sold as 1 A. Its own datasheet gives 90 °C/W for the SOT-223 package. Dropping 5 V to 3.3 V at 35 °C ambient, the arithmetic gives you about 425 mA before the junction passes 100 °C. The part was never lying. The heat sum just never got done.

Dropout and headroom

Every linear regulator needs the input to sit some minimum above the output. Below that it stops regulating and the output simply follows the input down.

A 7805 needs about 2 V, so it wants 7 V in for 5 V out. An AMS1117 needs 1.1–1.3 V. That is why an AMS1117 cannot make 3.3 V from a Li-ion cell — a cell at 3.7 V has only 0.4 V of headroom. It works on the bench from 5 V and fails on the battery, which is exactly the kind of failure that gets blamed on code.

Quiescent current

A regulator draws current simply to stay switched on, whether or not anything is using it. On mains this is irrelevant. On a battery that sleeps between readings, it is often the largest load in the circuit.

A 7805 draws around 5 mA doing nothing. Over a month that is roughly 3,600 mAh — more than most packs hold. An MCP1700 draws 1.6 µA, about three thousand times less. If your project sleeps, this number matters more than efficiency.

ESR — why capacitance is not the whole story

Every capacitor has a small resistance in series with it. Current through it makes a voltage drop, instantly, before the capacitor has released any charge at all.

A generic 1000 µF electrolytic sits around 0.15 Ω. Pull 2 A and you lose 300 mV immediately. If your whole droop budget was 350 mV, you have spent it on a parasitic and the capacitance never got a chance. Two parts both marked 10 µF can behave completely differently. Buy low-ESR, or put several in parallel.

Bursts: the regulator supplies the average, the capacitor supplies the burst

Radios, motors and cellular modules do not draw steady current — they spike. A GSM module pulls 2 A for 577 µs, over and over, while averaging nearer 300 mA.

A regulator's control loop takes tens of microseconds to react, so during a short burst it barely helps. The charge comes from a capacitor sitting next to the load: ΔV = (Ipeak − Ireg) × t ÷ C + Ipeak × ESR. You do not need a supply rated for the peak. You need a capacitor that can cover it — and it belongs beside the load, not beside the battery.

Cell resistance decides your peak current

Capacity tells you how long something runs. Internal resistance tells you whether it runs at all. Vsag = Ipeak × Rinternal.

Two amps through a good 18650 at 80 mΩ costs 160 mV — fine. The same 2 A through a CR2032 coin cell, which sits around 10–15 Ω, collapses it entirely. That is why an ESP32 on a coin cell resets the instant WiFi transmits. For pulsed loads, choose cells by resistance, not by mAh. A high-drain 18650 or an RC LiPo, not the cheapest cell on the shelf.

A boost converter draws more current than it delivers

Power in must exceed power out. So when a boost raises voltage, it lowers current on the way in — meaning the input current is higher than the output current, not lower.

5 V at 2 A is 10 W. From a 3.5 V cell at 90% efficiency, that needs about 3.2 A from the battery. Size the cell, the wiring and the fuse for that number. This single misunderstanding is why so many cheap boost modules disappoint: their headline rating is switch current, not output current.

When the input crosses the output

A buck only steps down. A boost only steps up. A 1S Li-ion runs from 4.2 V down to 3.0 V, so a 3.3 V rail from that cell needs both — buck at the start of the discharge, boost at the end.

That is what a buck-boost or SEPIC is for. But check first whether you need a regulator at all: if your load accepts a voltage range that contains the battery's range, connect it directly. Cellular modules want 3.4–4.2 V, almost exactly a Li-ion cell. No conversion means no loss, no switching noise and fewer parts.

Cells in series, and why 1S is the easy case

S counts cells in series and adds voltage: 1S Li-ion is 3.0–4.2 V, 2S is 6.0–8.4 V, 3S is 9.0–12.6 V. P counts cells in parallel and adds capacity and peak current.

Everything gets harder past 1S, because cells in series drift apart. One cell creeping high overcharges, which is a fire. One creeping low is a dead pack. So 1S needs nothing but a charger, while 2S and above needs a BMS that balances and a charger matched to the cell count.

Charging, and charging while running

Lithium cells charge at constant current until they reach their termination voltage, then hold that voltage while current tapers off. Stop at the wrong voltage and you either undercharge or damage the cell.

A TP4056 terminates at 4.2 V, correct for Li-ion. LiFePO4 must stop at 3.65 V — put a TP4056 on one and you overcharge it every cycle. And a plain TP4056 charges only: the load cannot run properly off the cell at the same time. Running while charging needs a power-path charger, which is a different part.

Simultaneity — what actually peaks together

Adding up every datasheet maximum gives a supply two or three times bigger than you need. Assuming only one thing peaks at a time can give you one that is half the size it should be.

The useful split is by behaviour. Motors and servos move together — four servos in an arm all draw at once, so sum their peaks. Radios transmit independently, so count one peak plus everything else's average. Three MG996R servos is a 7.5 A peak, not 3.5 A, and that difference is the whole design.

LDO stability and the output capacitor

An LDO is a feedback loop, and its output capacitor is part of that loop. Fit the wrong one and it oscillates — a supply that measures fine on a multimeter while ringing at hundreds of kilohertz.

Older LDOs need a minimum ESR and go unstable with a pure ceramic. Newer ones want the opposite. The datasheet states which capacitor type and value range it was designed for, and it is not a suggestion. This is one thing no calculator can check for you.

Wiring it, even on perfboard

The circuit that works is not the schematic — it is the schematic plus every wire's resistance and inductance. On perfboard, a few rules cover most of it.

Keep the high-current loop short and fat: 20 AWG, not jumper wire. Put bulk capacitance beside the load, not beside the battery. Ceramics closest to the pin, electrolytic behind them — small and fast in front, big and slow behind. Star ground at the source, so a motor's return current never travels through your microcontroller's ground.

Why a multimeter is not enough

A multimeter samples a few times a second and reports an average. The failures that matter last microseconds.

A 3.3 V rail collapsing to 2.6 V for 200 µs, two hundred times a second, still reads 3.29 V on a meter — which looks perfect. The chip resets anyway and you go hunting through your code. The meter is not lying; it is answering a different question. To know whether a supply works, put a scope on it, AC coupled with a short ground spring, and trigger on the falling edge.

Mains — the one place to be conservative

230 V will kill you, and perfboard has neither the creepage distance nor the mechanical protection to be safe with it. Never build a non-isolated mains circuit on perfboard.

There are four ways to get DC from mains, and for almost everyone the first is right: a certified adapter. Then a potted isolated module such as a Hi-Link. Then an offline switcher IC, if you want to build and understand it. The transformer-bridge-capacitor-7805 circuit from old textbooks is there to teach rectification and ripple — it is not a recommendation.
